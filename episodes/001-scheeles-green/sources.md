# Sources — Pilot #1: Scheele's Green
Verified via web search, 2026-10-25.

1. **Wikipedia — Scheele's green** — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scheele%27s_green
   - Invented in 1775 by Carl Wilhelm Scheele; chemically cupric hydrogen arsenite / copper arsenite (CuHAsO3); related to Paris Green.
   - Yellowish-green pigment, widely used early–mid 19th century in paints and as a colourant in many products; used as food dye for sweets such as green blancmange.
   - Reports of children wasting away in bright green rooms; acute poisoning of children at a Christmas party where dyed candles were burned.
   - Some European nations started banning arsenic-containing pigments in the 1830s and 1840s; it did not completely fall out of favour until the 1860s.
   - 1861 death of 19-year-old Matilda Scheueur, from dusting artificial foliage with the pigment; autopsy detailed eyes and fingernails turned green.
   - By the 1890s the last brand of wallpaper using it ceased production.
   - Wallpaper poisoning mechanisms: pigment flakes become airborne dust absorbed by lungs; damp/mouldy wallpaper can be metabolised by fungi (e.g. Scopulariopsis) releasing poisonous arsine / methyl arsine gas ("Gosio gas").

2. **Actalent — Historic Chemical Substitutions, Part 3: Arsenic Green** — https://www.Actalentservices.com/en-gb/insights/articles/historic-chemical-substitutions-part-three-arsenic-green
   - Corroborates "wallpaper poisoning" from arsenic-laden dust and gases, especially as surfaces deteriorated or became mouldy.
   - States arsenic green was never officially banned overall, but was completely replaced by less toxic paints in the 19th century — hence the script's wording ("some countries banned… Britain just argued… makers quietly stopped") rather than a blanket ban claim.
