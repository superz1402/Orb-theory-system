London, 1861. A mother hangs the most fashionable colour in England in her baby's nursery.

The paper goes up beautifully. It dries beautifully. By winter, it is the most dangerous object in the house.

She has no idea what is in it. Neither does the baby.

It will not kill them quickly. That was never its method. It kills slowly — and, eventually, in writing.

I am the Archivist. I keep the files on beautiful things that hurt people. File number one — is a colour.

Our story starts in 1775, in a Swedish pharmacy, with a chemist named Carl Wilhelm Scheele.

Scheele was a genius of a particular, self-destructive kind. He discovered element after element — oxygen among them — and then, characteristically, published too late to get the credit.

History's most productive man at losing priority. I keep a whole shelf for him.

What he wanted, in 1775, was a green that would stay green. Every green pigment of the age faded, yellowed, or died in sunlight.

So he built one. Copper. Arsenic. A compound the chemists write as CuHAsO3 — cupric hydrogen arsenite.

And the colour — the colour was extraordinary. A green so bright it seemed lit from inside the paper.

He had invented the most beautiful poison in Europe. He simply hadn't framed it that way.

Europe did not adopt Scheele's green. It surrendered to it.

Within a generation it was the colour of respectability: wallpaper, book bindings, dresses, gloves, candles, cake decorations — even green blancmange, served for dessert.

The Victorians did not have a death wish. They had a decorating scheme.

And it was cheap to produce. That was the engine of it — the colour of luxury, at a price that put it in every parlour in Europe.

By 1858, London papers were reporting the scale of it: one maker, in one city, using two tons of arsenic a week — just for wallpaper.

Two tons. A single square foot of that paper, the reports added, held enough arsenic to poison five or six people.

Nobody stopped buying it. Green rooms were the fashion, and fashion — as this archive keeps demonstrating — outranks evidence.

So how does a wall poison a family? Slowly, and by two roads. Watch the wall.

Road one is dust. The pigment was never truly fixed in the paper. It shed — invisibly, constantly — fine green dust that settled on floors, on toys, on skin, and was breathed in, a little, every day.

Road two is worse. It begins when the room gets damp.

Moisture creeps through the plaster. Mould takes hold in the paste beneath the paper — quietly, where no one can see it.

And the mould does something no chemist in 1775 could have predicted. It feeds on the pigment — and exhales it. A poisonous arsenic gas, drifting off the walls into the room's air.

Later science would give the gas a name: Gosio gas. The nurseries of England were breathing it nightly.

The wall was not decorated. It was loaded.

The dose was tiny. That is precisely the horror of the file. Tiny — every hour, every night, for years.

Children sickened first. In bright green rooms, in the best houses, children began — in the reports' own terrible phrase — wasting away.

The archive keeps one name above all the case files. Matilda Scheueur. Nineteen years old.

Matilda did not hang wallpaper. She manufactured the greenery for it — dusting artificial leaves with the green powder, all day, every day, with her hands.

What drifted off a family's walls in a year settled on her hands, her clothes, her lungs — every single day.

In 1861 she died of it. The doctors who examined her recorded a detail that belongs in this file, because it is the colour signing its own work.

Her eyes had turned green. So had her fingernails.

Nineteen. The archive has older paper than her whole life.

Matilda's death was not hidden. It was reported. She was simply — filed under acceptable.

Because by then, the warnings were no longer whispers. Doctors were writing case reports. The medical press was connecting green rooms to sick children, green candles to poisoned parties.

At one Christmas party, children were acutely poisoned where candles dyed with the green had been burning. The candles. At a children's party.

And in 1862, Punch magazine turned the whole scandal into a cartoon: two skeletons, dressed for a ball, dancing. The Arsenic Waltz.

A joke is what a culture makes when the truth has arrived and nobody intends to act on it.

The knowledge was public. The mechanism was published. The dead were named.

And the paper kept selling. Which tells you the problem was never information.

What the file needed was not another warning. It needed evidence a family could hold. In 1874, it got one.

Doctor Robert C. Kedzie, an American physician, collected actual samples of the arsenical wallpapers — the real papers, cut from real rolls — and bound them into a book.

He titled it Shadows from the Walls of Death. And he subtitled it, with a librarian's precision: facts and inferences, prefacing a book of specimens of arsenical wall papers.

Think about what that book is. Not an argument. An evidence locker. Page after page of the poison itself, filed between covers, sent to libraries so the public could see the thing that hung in their own halls.

Curators do not exaggerate. Physicians, it turns out, file accurately.

A book whose pages were, themselves, the poison it described.

That is the "in writing" I promised you, back in the nursery. The colour, documented. The dust, documented. The dead, documented.

So the state banned it, the factories closed, and the story ends. That is how this story is usually told. It is not what the file says.

Here is what the file says. In the 1830s and 1840s — decades before Kedzie — some European nations had already begun banning arsenic pigments.

Britain, the greenest nation of all, debated. Committees met. Reports were written. The colour stayed on sale.

There was never a British ban. Say it plainly, because the myth is tidier than the truth: never banned.

What killed Scheele's green was not law. It was embarrassment. By the 1860s the colour was falling out of favour; by the 1890s, the last brand of wallpaper made with it had quietly ceased production.

And the industry that had sold poison as elegance discovered a new selling point, printed right on the advert: free from arsenic.

Imagine having to advertise that. Imagine it working.

The colour is gone now. The walls it covered are gone, most of them, and good riddance filed alongside.

But the pattern in this file is not dated 1861. It is the pattern of every beautiful, cheap, celebrated thing whose bill arrives slowly, and in writing.

Check what your walls are made of. The archive certainly will.
